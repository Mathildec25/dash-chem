Human-in-the-loop Bayesian optimisation · weekly report

Week of 7–13 September 2026

I started the week with an emulator that did not run, a trigger I could not justify and no way to show any of it to a chemist. I ended it with three reactions, a trigger whose threshold means the same thing at any budget, a null model measured on forty branches, and a REACTO page on which my colleagues can be handed a campaign that Bayesian optimisation gets wrong. This is how one led to the next.

Monday 7 — where to build, and on what

Can I trust the benchmark I already have, and where should the study live?

My earlier bench ran on Olympus emulators of the Reizman Suzuki campaigns, in its own folder, with its own copy of the optimisation code and a Python environment that turned out to be dead. Repairing Olympus took three patches before it ran at all. The question that settled things was simpler than repairing anything: Minerva already publishes the emulators evaluated on a complete grid (5,670 conditions per case, yields included). A grid needs no emulator, gives an exact Pareto front, and makes a campaign reproducible to the decimal. We checked the four grids against the repaired Olympus (r ≥ 0.997) and kept them.

The second decision followed from the first. If the study is about what my optimiser does, the bench must call it, not a copy. So the work moved into REACTO's own repository and imports its bayesian_optimization(); Olympus, TensorFlow 1.15 and the extra environment left with the old folder. By the evening, five case II campaigns had reproduced the bimodality I knew from my notebook — 37–56 % of the front when the optimiser settles on XPhos, 97–100 % when it finds PCy3 — on the real tool this time.

What changed · One optimiser, one repository, exact fronts. Everything measured after this point is measured on the thing the chemists will use.

Tuesday 8 — can a signal tell a trapped campaign from a good one?

My trigger fired on a cadence; was it reading anything?

I had brought a trigger — recent gain over total gain, a threshold, a cooldown — and a family of alternatives (plateau length, the surrogate's over-optimism, collapsed uncertainty, the acquisition value). We measured all of them on the campaigns. None separates a campaign converging on the right catalyst from one converging on the wrong one. Progress, ligand coverage, concentration: identical in both; only the identity of the ligand differs, and that is what the campaign cannot know. Worse, my trigger's condition held in 46 % of experiments, so the cooldown, not the signal, set when it fired.

That result changed what I could ask of a trigger. If nothing diagnoses, the trigger cannot decide; it can only book an appointment with someone who can. The asymmetry makes that acceptable: interrupting a healthy campaign costs one experiment in thirty, missing a trapped one costs fifty points of front. We kept my signal's shape but corrected its denominator — recent pace over the campaign's own average pace, P* — because the original ratio decays on its own under steady progress and its threshold silently encodes a firing time.

What changed · The trigger books, the chemist diagnoses. The study's question moved from “can we detect a trap?” to “what does a chemist do when called?”

Wednesday 9 — which reactions, and what the chemist may answer

Suzuki case II was the only trap I knew. Was it the only one worth showing?

Overnight, nine more complete grids were screened with the same optimiser. I did not want the reaction where BO does best on average; I wanted the one where the outcome depends on the initial draw, because that is the only place a chemist can change something. The spread between the worst and the best seed became the selection criterion, with two hard constraints: a complete grid, and reagents with names a chemist can argue about.

Three survived: Suzuki II (63 points of spread, a categorical trap), Suzuki I (45), and the C–H arylation of EDBO+ — 1,728 conditions all measured at Bristol Myers Squibb, with two independent objectives, yield and reagent cost, where the Suzuki pair is redundant. I decided to stop working on Suzuki alone: it had given what it could.

The same day I refused a fixed intervention schedule (“imagine it falls exactly when the optimiser is progressing well”), and added the answer that had been missing: at an alert the chemist may propose a point, let the optimiser continue, or stop the campaign. Stopping is a decision only a human can take — we measured that stopping at the first alert saves twenty experiments and loses a third of the hypervolume — and the distribution of the three answers is itself a result.

What changed · Three reactions, three answers. The human study now has a shape.

Thursday 10 — freezing the trigger, and a bug that would have voided the design

With the trigger's form settled, what window, what threshold, and is any of this reproducible?

We plotted where P fires on every campaign for each candidate setting, and I read the plots against a rule I had not written down before: after the ten initial points there must be five optimiser proposals before the trigger may speak — a chemist is not asked to judge three experiments. Then window and threshold together: at W = 5, a threshold of 0.10 left four campaigns silent, two of them failures; 0.30 alerted 13 of 13 failed case II campaigns at 2.5 solicitations each, first alert at 22.6 with a quarter of the gain still to come, and the one campaign never interrupted was the one that reached 100 %. I hesitated between my original ratio and P — they detect the same campaigns — and kept P* because its threshold does not change meaning with the budget. W = 5, 0.30, cooldown 5.

I also stated the research question as I wanted it answered — does intervening when the trigger says so improve a campaign, in time and in quality? — which fixed the design: three arms sharing the same history up to the alert, plain BO, a random point at every alert (the null model), a chemist's point at every alert. A paired design like that only works if a fork without intervention reproduces its parent. It did not. I asked why, since one can seed BO. The cause was in REACTO: the BoFire strategy was built without a seed and drew one from operating-system entropy at every step. Fixed that afternoon, with a seed derived from the campaign and the iteration.

What changed · Frozen trigger, three-arm design, campaigns that replay to twelve decimals. The bench could finally compute the two arms that need no chemist.

Thursday night to Friday — what chance achieves at the alerts

Before asking chemists to beat the trigger, what does a random point at the same moments do?

Twenty arylation controls and forty random branches, 130 interventions, each branch compared with its own parent. Paired gain +1.3 points; 18 branches of 40 improve, Wilson [31 %, 60 %]; range −27 to +23; no change in time to 90 %. A random point moves a campaign a lot in both directions and on average almost not at all. That is the null I wanted: if the chemists beat it, it is expertise, not the effect of a shake. The only gain in it was luck on the ligand — one injected point in five hit a front ligand and those gained three times more.

The number that stayed with me is not +1.3 but how it got there: +4.0 on eleven branches, +2.7 on nineteen, +1.3 on forty. A human arm with five chemists lives in that regime. I will not conclude on a mean over a dozen answers; the comparison has to be paired, on the same checkpoints, against this arm.

Friday went to the question I had been circling since Tuesday: how do chemists who do not code take part? An HTML form, an invitation page and a Colab notebook were all built and all rejected. The answer was the one I had rejected first for being more work: a page inside REACTO, where a chemist types a name, watches the initial design, launches the optimiser, and is stopped by the trigger.

What changed · The null is nearly zero. The chemists' page exists as a tested engine with a thin interface.

Saturday 12 — what exactly are the chemists being asked to overturn?

When I say seed 2 is trapped — is that the initial design, or the optimiser's own randomness?

One integer had always seeded both. With the acquisition now seedable we held the initial design and varied the rest: arylation seed 2 ended at 72.5 % of the front four times out of four; Suzuki II seed 3 at 37.3 % four times out of four, one visit to PCy3 in thirty experiments each time. Given this optimiser, a campaign's fate is a function of its ten starting points. That made the article's frame obvious to me: not “does HITL help on average”, which five chemists cannot carry, but a case study on trapped initial designs — base rate from the twenty seeds, then one trapped design per reaction, and three paired branches. Does a chemist called between experiments 15 and 22 get the campaign out of a trap that neither the optimiser nor chance leaves?

So we laid out all twenty controls per reaction, with what the initial design had shown of the winning ligand, and I chose: Suzuki II seed 12 (44 %, alert at 16, PCy3 seen once at 0 %), Suzuki I seed 8 (69 %, alert at 20, Xantphos seen at 40 °C, rediscovered too late), arylation seed 2 (72 %, alert at 16, a good ligand found at once and then nothing). Three different ways to be wrong.

The rest of the day was spent being the first chemist. Opening the page in a browser exposed what HTTP tests could not (a callback the renderer refused, a race between ticks, catalyst codes in the menus where the table showed names); I asked for English, for the table to come first, for a resume list like the Optimization page, and for the chemistry — the reaction, the ligands drawn, the conditions, the prices per gram behind the cost objective — to be the first thing on screen, from the source figures rather than redrawn. Running the three campaigns myself showed the last gap: I never filled in why. The field is optional; it should not be.

What changed · The study is now a question I can state in one sentence, on three campaigns I can name, on a page I have used.
